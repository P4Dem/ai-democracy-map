P4Dem’s Democracy Framework

Author: Karoline Helbig
Version 0.5 – 21.08.2026
Introduction
The body of this codebook is the working instrument behind the democracy aspects coding for our AI-Democracy Landscape (see Step 3 of the process description). All entries in the database (threats, threat-and-mitigation pairings, or independent pro-democracy opportunities) are coded against these categories to establish which dimensions of democracy they affect. That coding is what allows the map to be read analytically rather than merely browsed: it makes it possible to ask which democratic domains are most exposed to AI-related threats, and which threats are rarely or never matched with mitigation strategies in the source material. Here we set out the categories themselves in full.
The framework adapts the International IDEA Democracy Assessment Framework (Beetham et al., 2008), which supplied the four-pillar structure and the sub-category names. IDEA's guide was written for measuring the democratic quality of countries; ours is conceptualised to identify the aspects of democracy that are affected in the context of AI use. We therefore extended and adjusted the original framework by International IDEA to our purpose. The definitions, conceptual rationale, decision rules for ambiguous cases and worked examples below were developed specifically for this project about AI and democracy. The rationale for each of our changes is set out in the process description (link). The results describe how AI's democratic consequences are discussed in our sources, not the state of democracy itself.

Source: Beetham, D., Carvalho, E., Landman, T., & Weir, S. (2008). Beetham, David et al. 2008 Assessing the Quality of Democracy: A Practical Guide.pdf. International IDEA. Available under: https://www.idea.int/sites/default/files/publications/assessing-the-quality-of-democracy-a-practical-guide.pdf.

Code Descriptions (Detailed)
1. Citizenship, Law and Rights
Concerns the rights of citizens and the state's ability to guarantee equal rights through constitutional and legal processes — including civil, political, economic and social rights.
1.1 Nationhood and Citizenship
Effects on the inclusivity of citizenship and the equal recognition and protection of minorities, vulnerable groups, and those at the margins of political life — including whether all people are equally seen, served, and protected by the state, or whether existing patterns of exclusion and discrimination are entrenched or deepened.
Code here when the item affects who is recognised and treated as a full citizen or rights-holder, creates differential visibility or protection across groups, or produces or reinforces arbitrary discrimination against minorities or marginalised populations.
Examples: Unequal trust in and uptake of AI tools among women, disabled individuals and other marginalised groups threatens to weaken AI's democratic potential if participation is uneven across populations; sexualised deepfakes and other attacks on female politicians can create a chilling effect that discourages women from running for office; minorities remain invisible to computer vision systems, reducing their representation and access to services.

1.2 Rule of Law and Access to Justice
Effects on the integrity of legal processes, judicial independence, and citizens' ability to obtain fair treatment and redress; on whether public officials and institutions are subject to transparent and consistently applied rules; and on whether accountability for harms is clear and enforceable.
Code here when an item affects legal processes or judicial independence, citizens' ability to obtain fair treatment or redress, or when accountability for harms caused by AI systems is unclear or absent.
Examples: Historically marginalised groups being overrepresented in crime records, negatively impacting group members in AI-based approaches to policing or sentencing; systematic biases in AI-supported policing and sentencing coming to systematically bias the electorate against historically disenfranchised groups; unclear legal liability for harms caused by AI-operated devices or services risks reducing accountability and undermining public trust in the technology.

1.3 Civil and Political Rights
Effects on the freedoms of expression, movement, association, and assembly; on freedom from physical violation or fear; on freedom from surveillance, profiling, and censorship; and on the protection of those who defend human rights and democratic values.
Code here when an item involves surveillance, profiling, censorship, or intimidation of citizens or civil society; restricts freedom of expression or assembly; or silences individuals on the basis of their beliefs or political activity.
Examples: AI-assisted government repression manifesting in enhanced surveillance, online censorship, and targeted persecution of online users; AI enabling face recognition, online tracking and profiling, and merging data in ways that produce results the person would not expect; AI tracking and profiling individuals linked to certain beliefs or actions, harming freedom of assembly and protest.

1.4 Economic and Social Rights
Effects on access to work, social security, and basic necessities; on health and education; and on the broad and equitable distribution of economic gains across society.
Code here when an item affects material conditions, labour rights, access to essential services, or the distribution of economic power and wealth — including where AI concentrates economic gains in the hands of a few or displaces workers from their livelihoods.
Note for coders: Items about regulating AI companies will often warrant multiple codes. Code 1.4 when the regulatory concern is primarily about material conditions or the distribution of economic gains. Where the concern is about corporate control over government infrastructure or state functions, add 2.3. Where it is about corporate influence over public policy or the regulatory process itself, add 2.7. A single item may legitimately carry all three codes.
Examples: AI substituting human labour and concentrating economic gains, exacerbating inequality; Economic disruption, wealth concentration and job displacement risking democratic backsliding; "AI helping ageing societies maintain productivity by completing substitutable work tasks
2. Representative and Accountable Government
Concerns the institutions and processes of representative and accountable government: electoral processes and political parties; the effectiveness, transparency, and integrity of public administration, legislature and judiciary; civilian control of security forces; and the absence of illegitimate influence on policy-making.
2.1 Free and Fair Elections
Effects on the integrity of electoral processes, the inclusivity and accessibility of voting, the fairness of political competition, and public trust in elections as genuinely open contests.
Code here when an item affects electoral integrity, voter access, campaign fairness, or public confidence in election outcomes.
Examples: Deepfakes and misinformation threatening to cause more extreme damage to electoral integrity; LLMs sharing incorrect or outdated voter registration information potentially contributing to voter suppression; AI prediction tools threatening to undermine public trust in elections as genuinely open contests.
2.2 The Democratic Role of Political Parties
Effects on the freedom of parties to form, recruit and campaign; and on whether political competition between parties remains fair and free from undue external influence or structural advantage.
Code here when an item affects how parties organise, compete or finance themselves, or where AI gives some parties a structural advantage over others.
Examples: AI-enabled voter outreach and fundraising levelling the playing field for campaigns that cannot afford expensive consultants; early access to AI giving campaigns a fleeting competitive advantage; candidates and campaigns using AI to build political bot networks and generate deepfakes impersonating opponents, distorting fair competition.
2.3 Effective and Accountable Government
Effects on the capacity of government to deliver public services fairly and implement policy faithfully; on the competence, transparency, and integrity of public administration; and on whether the systems and technologies governments use to exercise state power remain open to democratic scrutiny — including where dependencies on private or foreign providers shift effective control over state functions beyond democratic reach.
Code here when an item affects how well government serves citizens equitably, how faithfully it carries out its democratic mandate, how honestly and transparently it administers public affairs, or whether the operational tools of government — AI systems, data infrastructure, decision-support platforms — remain answerable to democratic institutions rather than to private interests.
Examples: Covert use of generative AI to write laws undermining transparency and accountability in the legislative process; The power to innovate and critically interrogate AI may shift from public to commercial actors, weakening oversight by democratically legitimated institutions
2.4 The Democratic Effectiveness of the Legislature
Effects on the capacity of parliament and other democratic institutions to scrutinise legislation and hold the executive to account; on the independence of the legislature from the executive; and on their capacity to receive and process genuine input.
Code here when an item affects legislative scrutiny, parliamentary oversight capacity, the balance of power between executive and legislature, or the ability of democratic institutions to remain genuinely accessible and responsive to citizen input.
Example: Mass AI-generated submissions overwhelming agencies, undermining their capacity to receive and process genuine citizen input.
2.5 Democratic Role of the Judiciary
Effects on the capacity of courts to provide independent oversight of the executive and legislature; on judicial review of laws and government decisions; and on the ability of citizens to challenge the exercise of public power through legal means.
Code here when an item affects judicial independence, constitutional review of AI-related legislation or government decisions, or citizens' ability to seek legal redress against automated or AI-assisted exercises of state power.
Examples: AI systems producing post-hoc explanations that do not faithfully reflect their actual decision process, preventing courts from meaningfully reviewing AI-assisted government decisions; courts needing institutional safeguards and capability thresholds to retain their oversight function as AI enters judicial work; judicial review of AI-enabled mass surveillance programmes — not yet represented in the dataset.

2.6 National–Subnational Power Relations
Effects on the independence and capacity of sub-central tiers of government; on local accountability and responsiveness to citizens; and on the distribution of power and resources between central and local government.
Code here when an item explicitly affects the balance of power between central and local government — including where AI-driven centralisation of data or infrastructure shifts effective power upward — or where it affects the ability of local or regional government to act independently and remain accountable to local communities. Where the concern is primarily about government capacity or service delivery rather than the central-local power balance, prefer 2.3.
Example: (Under-represented in the current dataset.) 

2.7 Civilian Control of the Military and Police
Effective civilian control over armed forces and security services; public accountability of police; absence of unaccountable private security actors.
Code here when AI changes what security or enforcement agencies can do, what civilians and oversight bodies can see or check about them, or who commands them — i.e. when the vertical accountability relationship between civilians/democratic institutions and security services is affected. [Do not code here for external cyberattacks on government infrastructure or for AI capability leaking to non-state actors; those are security problems, not civilian-control problems, and belong under 2.3, 1.2, or 4.1.]
Example: AI-enabled surveillance and facial recognition expanding police capabilities faster than civilian oversight institutions can audit them; AI-driven predictive enforcement enabling more targeted suppression of political dissent; state security services using AI for persecution and repression of citizens; biased AI systems in policing and sentencing producing discriminatory enforcement outcomes that civilian accountability mechanisms struggle to catch.
2.8 Illegitimate influence over policy-making
Effects on the extent to which powerful actors shape policy through back channels — such as hidden lobbying, capturing regulators, or flooding consultations with artificial responses — rather than through open and accountable processes, and on how far policy outputs and outcomes reflect what the public actually wants.
Code here when an item shows AI enabling or amplifying corruption, conflicts of interest, or non-transparent private influence over public policy; when it allows well-resourced actors to shape regulation, legislation, or public consultations through back channels, manufactured consensus, or lobbying at scale; or when it gives private interests disproportionate influence over individual officials or the policy process more broadly.
Examples: AI enabling targeted political persuasion by predicting individual reactions to communicative interventions, allowing professional communicators and lobbyists to influence citizens and legislators at scale; AI increasing the rhetorical and practical power of experts, reducing the option space available for democratic decision-making; growing government dependence on AI companies and enabling an opaque transfer of knowledge from governments to these service providers, concentrating structural power in private hands
3. Civil Society and Popular Participation
Concerns the conditions for an alert and active citizen body: the independence and viability of professional journalism; and the formation, exchange, and contestation of political opinion.
3.1 Independent and Fact-Based Journalistic Media
Effects on the independence and pluralism of regulated, professional journalism; on the representativeness of opinion and the investigative capacity of journalism; on journalists' ability to operate freely; and on the accountability of platforms for the information flows they shape and amplify.
Code here when an item affects the conditions under which regulated, professionally produced journalism operates — including its independence, revenue, ownership, investigative capacity, or distribution reach — or when it affects the accountability of platforms specifically in their role as distributors of journalistic content.
Examples: pressure on news organisations' revenues through AI-generated content and ultra-low-cost competitors, reducing the volume and diversity of political information; reduced traffic and revenue for smaller outlets through AI-powered search engines bypassing news links  concentrating media power in established brands; AI driving socio-economic inequality in access to reliable political information as quality journalism retreats behind paywalls.
3.2 Opinion Formation and Political Participation
Effects on the conditions under which citizens form, exchange, and revise political views — including through social media and other non-journalistic channels;  on the depth, breadth, and authenticity of citizen participation in public life; on equal access to public voice across social groups; and on the range and independence of civil society and social movements.
Code here when an item affects how citizens form or express political views, organise collectively, or participate in public life; when AI shapes the information environment through which public opinion is formed, including social media, persuasion, disinformation, and echo chambers; or when it affects the capacity of civil society to act independently and authentically.
Examples: AI-generated content, LLM agents, and bots shaping or eroding the online opinion environment; AI supporting new deliberative and participatory formats; social activists using AI to improve and tailor messaging.

4. Transnational Dynamics
Concerns the international dimensions of democracy: external influences on a country's democracy, and the country's democratic impact abroad.
4.1 External Influences on the Country's Democracy
Effects on a country's freedom from external interference in its democratic processes; on the geopolitical balance between democratic and autocratic models of governance; and on the ability of democratic states to maintain autonomous control over their political institutions and infrastructure.
Code here when an item involves AI-enabled foreign interference in domestic democratic processes, or where the competitive advantage of AI-enabled autocracies — in governance capacity, surveillance, or information operations — affects the broader democratic-autocratic balance.
Examples: AI enabling foreign interference in elections; AI-assisted governance increasing autocratic state capacity and service quality, potentially strengthening public support for authoritarian systems at democracy's expense.
4.2 The Country's Democratic Impact Abroad
Effects on a country's support for human rights and democracy in other countries; on respect for international rule of law; and on whether a country's policies, exports, and international conduct strengthen or undermine democratic governance elsewhere.
Code here when AI affects how the coded country shapes democratic conditions outside its borders — through export of AI tools, participation in global AI governance, or setting regulatory precedents other states follow.
Examples: urging governments to impose sanctions on entities enabling AI-facilitated repression and pressuring technology companies to cease AI sales to authoritarian regimes
